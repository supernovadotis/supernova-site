*We can reshape climate, ecosystems and information environments, and steer none of them. The form of responsibility that fits that situation already exists. It has just never been scaled up.*

## A rule that worked

On 1 January 2020 the limit on sulphur in ships' fuel oil fell from 3.50% to 0.50% outside designated control areas. The decision had been taken in 2008 and confirmed in 2016, written into Annex VI of the MARPOL convention. The reason was lungs. Sulphur oxides cause respiratory and cardiovascular disease, and the people breathing ship exhaust live in ports and along coasts. By the International Maritime Organization's own account, the new limit cut total sulphur oxide emissions from shipping by about 70%.

It worked. It also did something nobody had on the agenda.

For a century, ship exhaust had been seeding bright low clouds over the oceans — the ship tracks you can see from orbit. Bright clouds reflect sunlight. In May 2024, a team of eleven led from NASA Goddard by Tianle Yuan published its reading of the satellite record of what happened when the sulphur stopped: the regulation had abruptly ended a cooling effect nobody had designed. They call it an "inadvertent geoengineering termination shock."

Read the sequence slowly. The right body acted, through the right process, on good evidence, for a defensible reason, and produced a planetary-scale side effect that was outside its mandate, outside its expertise, and outside anyone's power to reverse on request. There is no villain in this story and no error. There is a decision, an effect, and no steering.

That is the situation this publication is about, and it is not confined to the atmosphere.

In ecosystems the pattern has a name and a literature. In 1996 the ecologist C. S. Holling, writing with Gary Meffe in *Conservation Biology*, described what he called the pathology of natural resource management: agencies meet a problem by controlling it, the control reduces the natural variation in the system, the system becomes less resilient, and the result is "unforeseen consequences for both natural ecosystems and human welfare." Then the agency responds to the surprise with more control. Holling was not an outside critic of the field; he had spent his career inside it, and the paper reads as a diagnosis from within.

In the information environment the clearest documented case is also the best intentioned. In January 2018 Facebook rewrote the ranking of its News Feed to favour what the company called meaningful social interactions — more from friends and family, less professionally produced content, on the evidence that the former was better for users. Internal memos later obtained by the *Wall Street Journal* record the company's own researchers concluding that the change had made the platform "an angrier place," because the metric rewarded reshares, and the content that gets reshared most is the content that provokes. The documents are Facebook's own; the people describing the failure were the people running the system.

Three domains, three named cases, one structure. In each, a specific body with real authority made a decision it was entitled to make, and the consequence arrived from a direction nobody was facing. And in each, the question *who is in charge of this?* has no answer — not because the answer is hidden, but because there is nothing in the position.

## What every theory of maturity assumes

Set that beside any available account of what it means to be grown up.

The most widely used map of adult development is Robert Kegan's. In *The Evolving Self* and *In Over Our Heads* he describes a sequence of orders of mind, and the one that most training, coaching and leadership work is trying to produce is the fourth: the self-authoring mind. Its mark is that you stop being written by your context and start writing. You hold your own frame, generate your own standard, and act from it.

It is a good description of something real. It is also, structurally, a theory about control — about moving the locus of authorship inside. Organisational and political theories of maturity do the same work with different vocabulary: the mature institution sets objectives and delivers against them; the mature state is the one that can carry out what it decides.

Underneath all of them sits an assumption that philosophy has stated more precisely than management ever has. Bernard Williams in 1976 and Thomas Nagel in 1979 gave it a name in a pair of essays both titled "Moral Luck": the control principle, which holds that we are morally assessable only for what depends on factors within our control. Williams expected the phrase moral luck to read as an oxymoron. The point of both essays is that the principle, which is very hard to give up, does not survive contact with how we actually judge. We assess people constantly for what they did not control.

Williams and Nagel were arguing about blame for things that have already happened. The situation in front of us is the forward-facing version of the same problem, and it is worse. Not an agent judged for an outcome luck interfered with, but a whole class of decisions where the outcome was never on the table.

## Two answers, both young

Faced with that, two responses are available, and both are in wide circulation.

The first says control is temporarily missing and will be restored by better technology. Its most literal expression to date is a two-person startup called Make Sunsets, which released weather balloons of sulphur dioxide over Baja California Sur — first reported by *MIT Technology Review* in December 2022 — and sold what it called cooling credits by the gram. No notice to the Mexican government, no consent from the communities below. On 13 January 2023 Mexico's environment ministry announced it would prohibit solar geoengineering experimentation in the country, and went on to press other states to do the same. The founder's defence, given to Reuters by email, was that the responsible experts were not solving the problem.

The structure of that answer is worth naming precisely, because it is not stupid. It is the belief that the gap between impact and steering is a technical gap, and that the correct response to not being in charge is to acquire the instrument that puts you in charge. What the episode demonstrated instead is that a thermostat in private hands is not control. It is one more uncoordinated impact, with a price list.

The second answer says that if nothing can be steered, nothing need be attempted. Its most-read statement is Jem Bendell's 2018 paper "Deep Adaptation: A Map for Navigating Climate Tragedy," written while he was a professor at the University of Cumbria and released by his own institute there. The paper's own opening note records that it was rejected by reviewers at a journal who had asked for major changes. It is premised on "near term social collapse" being inevitable, and argues that existing work on climate adaptation is therefore built on a false floor. It reached an audience peer-reviewed climate literature does not reach, which says something about the demand.

The science in it has been argued over at length elsewhere, and that argument is not this essay's. What matters here is the inference readers commonly draw, which is frequently not the one the paper asks for: if the outcome is fixed, then action is theatre, and the honest posture is withdrawal.

Both answers do the same thing. They keep control as the condition of responsibility, and then split on whether control is coming. One waits for the instrument. The other grieves its absence. Neither has to work out what you owe in the meantime, which is why both are restful.

## The third form already exists

There is a third position, and the reason to take it seriously is that it is not a proposal. It is a description of how responsibility is already constituted in several fields where outcomes have never been controllable.

Hans Jonas, writing in 1979, grounded responsibility not in control but in power — in the capacity to affect. Having the power entails the responsibility, whatever the source of the power. And he located the origin of the whole structure in a relation where control is conspicuously absent. The section heading in *The Imperative of Responsibility* is blunt: "Parent-Child Relation: The Archetype of Responsibility." What he calls "the timeless archetype of all responsibility, the parental for the child" is non-reciprocal, unchosen, and makes its claim through the sheer vulnerability of the newborn. Jonas was a philosopher rather than a practitioner of anything; this is a view from outside, and it was formulated in response to exactly the predicament we are in, which he described as a gap between the power to act and the ability to foresee.

The law has gone further than the philosophy, because the law had to adjudicate cases.

Consider how a trustee is judged. Under the Uniform Prudent Investor Act, adopted in most American states, compliance with the prudent investor rule is "determined in light of the facts and circumstances existing at the time" of the trustee's decision — and expressly not by hindsight. The trustee cannot control the market. The trustee is therefore not assessed on the return. The assessed object is the quality of the decision at the moment it was made, with the information then available.

Consider how a decision is judged when it is made for someone who cannot make it themselves. Under the UK's Mental Capacity Act 2005, every act done for a person lacking capacity must be in that person's best interests; section 4 sets out what the decision-maker must consider and declines to define best interests exhaustively; and subsection 9 protects the person who acts in the reasonable belief that they are acting in those interests. The outcome may be bad. The decision may still have been right. The two questions are separated in statute.

Medicine runs on the same separation, which is why the duty is a duty of care and not a warranty of cure. In English law the classical statement is Mr Justice McNair's direction in *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* in 1957: a doctor is not negligent if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of opinion in that field. The test has been qualified since, in *Bolitho* and then in *Montgomery* on consent, and the qualifications tightened the examination of the reasoning — not of the result.

So the form exists, it is old, and it is unsentimental. In each case: the agent has real power and no control; the responsibility is nonetheless assigned and enforceable; and what gets examined is the process, the information, the consideration of whose interests are at stake, and the willingness to revisit — never the outcome alone.

What has never happened is the application of that structure at civilisational scale. It governs a trustee and a ward, a doctor and a patient, a parent and a child. It does not govern a fuel standard with a cloud-albedo side effect, or a ranking change with a politics side effect.

One distinction, because the two cases get confused and this publication has already published on the other one. Responsibilization is the move that shifts a duty from an institution onto a person who lacks the means to discharge it; Iris Marion Young's separation of liability from political connection is the tool for telling such cases apart. That case is about control that exists, is being exercised, and sits somewhere other than with the person being held to account. This one is about the absence of control as such. The first is a misallocation and can be corrected. The second is a condition and has to be inhabited.

## What it looks like with a budget

Wales is the furthest anyone has taken this into institutional form, which is why it belongs here as a case and not as an aspiration.

The Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015 requires listed public bodies to pursue seven well-being goals and, in doing so, to apply five ways of working: long term, prevention, integration, collaboration, involvement. Two of those five are the exact mechanisms this argument needs. *Long term* makes the far consequence of a near decision a thing the body must demonstrate it weighed. *Involvement* obliges it to involve people with an interest in the goals, reflecting the diversity of the area served — which is the statutory version of asking who is not in the room. The Act also created an office to hold the rest to it: the Future Generations Commissioner for Wales, a statutory guardian of the interests of people who do not yet exist, with reports, a remit and public scrutiny.

It is also where the asymmetry becomes visible. Giving evidence to the Senedd's Equality and Social Justice Committee, the current commissioner, Derek Walker, put his own position plainly: he is trying to guide a £30 billion public sector with a body funded at £1.6 million a year, which he compared to a sticking plaster on the Titanic. Committee witnesses have been blunter still about the Act's enforcement, and a Labour member, Mick Antoniw, told the same committee the law risks being a bureaucratic tick-box exercise. This is a process working as designed and reporting on its own insufficiency — which is what a procedure that does not promise outcomes looks like from inside. Walker speaks from the office; the committee is the outside check on it. Both records are public, and the full account belongs in its own piece.

Three practical commitments follow from the structure, and each has a working precedent.

**Decisions carry a review date.** Not an aspiration to revisit, a date. The Montreal Protocol has worked this way since 1990: Article 6 requires the parties to assess the control measures at least every four years on the basis of current scientific, environmental, technical and economic information, through expert panels convened for the purpose. The treaty's authority came from being revisable rather than from being right first time, and it has in fact been adjusted and amended repeatedly since — London, Copenhagen, Montreal, Beijing, Kigali. A decision with no review date is a claim to have predicted correctly.

**The question of who is not in the room is asked out loud, by procedure.** Wales puts it in statute. In the Haudenosaunee seven-generations tradition, as the explainer in this set argues, it is a sequence rather than a sentiment — and that is the step most often skipped. Gary Klein's premortem compresses the same move into half an hour of one planning meeting by assuming the project has already failed and asking the room what went wrong. Arnold Mindell's work on rank explains why the question is not asked spontaneously: the standing you hold in a room is the thing you cannot see while you hold it, and whoever is absent costs nothing to omit.

**Metrics that quietly presume steering are refused.** This is the hardest of the three, because such metrics are what funders and boards accept. The clearest live example is net zero: in 2021, James Dyke, Robert Watson and Wolfgang Knorr — Watson a former chair of the IPCC — argued in *The Conversation* that the concept had become a dangerous trap, licensing continued emissions against promises of future removal at a scale that does not exist. Whatever one makes of their conclusion, the structural point is the one that applies here. A target stated as a net outcome embeds an assumption that the balancing mechanism is available on demand. A commitment stated as a rate of reduction, by a named party, by a date, with a review, does not.

## The working definition

So: planetary adulthood, as this publication uses the term, is not a stage anyone reaches and not a set of beliefs about the future. It is a specific operating position — holding responsibility that does not rest on control, in the three domains where our power already exceeds our steering, using the procedural form that medicine, guardianship and trust law have used for a century, at a scale none of them were built for.

The eleven texts that follow this one at launch are the test of that definition. A law with an office and a budget. A procedure with a third step. A planning method that takes thirty minutes. A term for the standing that silences a room. Someone who holds a system together and appears on no ledger. Each is a case where the definition either does work or does not.

What is already settled is the premise. The power to reshape climate, ecosystems and information environments is distributed across thousands of bodies acting lawfully within their mandates, and the capacity to steer the result is held by none of them. That will not be fixed by a better instrument, and it is not dissolved by deciding the outcome is sealed. It leaves a form of responsibility that is assigned without being accompanied by control, which is the oldest kind there is, and the one nobody has yet built institutions for at this size.
