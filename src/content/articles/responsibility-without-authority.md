*Sociologists named it responsibilization: a duty moved off an institution and onto a person. In coaching, the same word names the opposite move. Telling the two apart is the whole skill.*

## Three instructions with the same grammar

You rinse the yoghurt pot before you put it in the right bin. Somewhere a container ship is burning fuel oil, and a chemical plant is discharging into a river under a permit, and you are rinsing a yoghurt pot.

Your division is cut. HR offers a reskilling stipend and a LinkedIn Learning licence. The market moved; you are told to move with it. Nobody mentions the capital allocation decision made two quarters ago in a room you have never been in.

You are exhausted. Your organisation introduces a wellbeing programme — breathing app, resilience webinar, a workshop on boundaries. Headcount is unchanged. The workload is unchanged. What's changed is that your exhaustion now has a name and the name points at you.

Three instructions with the same grammar. *The problem is systemic. The address is personal.*

## The term, and where it comes from

This is not a figure of speech. It is a described mechanism with roughly forty years of literature behind it.

Responsibilization names the process by which a person is made individually responsible for a task that used to be someone else's duty — usually a state agency's — or that nobody recognised as a duty at all. The lineage runs through [Nikolas Rose and Peter Miller](https://www.jstor.org/stable/591464), and behind them Foucault, on how modern power governs by shaping conduct rather than issuing orders.

David Garland put the word into circulation in 1996, [writing about crime control](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.bjc.a014105). His argument was that the normality of high crime rates, together with the evident limits of what criminal justice agencies could actually do, created a predicament for governments. Responsibilization was one of the adaptive answers — not more policing, but a redistribution of the task. Fit the alarm. Watch your street. Don't leave it on the seat.

Ronen Shamir generalised it beyond crime in [*The Age of Responsibilization*](https://doi.org/10.1080/03085140701760833) (*Economy and Society*, 2008). His formulation is the one worth carrying: where top-down bureaucracies ran on obedience, "responsibility is the practical master-key of governance." Not a softer version of the same power. A different key, which opens a different door, and opens it from the inside.

Michael Maniates had already shown what this does to politics. [Writing about environmentalism in 2001](https://doi.org/10.1162/152638001316881395), he described the individualization of responsibility and its cost: when responsibility for environmental problems is individualized, there is little room left to think about institutions, or about the nature and exercise of political power. That is the point. The mechanism does not only reassign the work. It removes the counterparty. If the problem is the sum of individual behaviours, there is nobody to negotiate with and nothing to legislate.

## Why it doesn't feel like coercion

Here is the part that matters most, and the part that keeps the mechanism running.

Responsibilization does not arrive as an order. It arrives as respect. You are treated as competent, informed, capable of judgement. You are handed a choice where previously you had a rule. The language is the language of autonomy, empowerment, ownership, agency — all the words you would use to describe a good thing happening to you.

That is not a side effect of the design. That *is* the design. A mechanism that worked by visible compulsion would generate visible resistance. This one works by producing people who ask it of themselves. The obligation is internalised before anyone has to enforce it, and when it fails, it fails inside you, privately, with your name on it.

Which is why the failure state is so specific. Not protest. Shame.

## The same word, the opposite move

Now the turn, because the word has a second life.

I work as a coach. In that room, "responsibilization" means almost exactly the reverse: giving a person back what they have handed over. Authorship. The right to decide. The recognition that inside even a tightly constrained situation there remains a real choice — not about outcomes, but about stance, about what you are willing to carry forward and on what terms. That work is my profession and how I earn a living, and you should read the next section knowing that.

So the same word covers two opposite operations: a burden pushed onto you without the means to carry it, and a capacity returned to you that was always yours. Both feel, from the inside, like being handed something. One of them hollows you out over eighteen months. The other is the only thing that makes the work survivable.

Intuition is a bad instrument for telling them apart, because both arrive dressed as trust.

## The test

Four questions. They are more reliable than the feeling in your stomach.

**Do I have the means?** Not willpower — means. Budget, headcount, time, access, information. If the task requires a resource you cannot obtain, you have not been given a task. You have been given an outcome you will be measured against.

**Who still gets to decide?** If the choice that determines the result was made elsewhere and is not reopenable, your responsibility is for execution within someone else's frame. That can be legitimate. It should be named.

**What happens if I decline?** Responsibility you cannot refuse is not responsibility. It is assignment. A genuine handover includes a real no — one with a survivable cost.

**Did the authority come with it?** This is the load-bearing question. Were you given the power to act on what you are now accountable for, or only the accountability?

Four yeses: it's yours. Take it.

Any no: something has been moved onto you that didn't come with what it needs.

## Responsibility without authority — and the limits of saying so

The blunt version, which the whole piece runs into:

**Responsibility without authority is not trust. It is a pre-assigned culprit.**

Someone has to absorb the gap between what the system promises and what it can deliver. Responsibilization decides, in advance, who that will be. Not by malice, usually — by drift, by the path of least institutional resistance. But the structure is the structure. When the result fails, the failure has a location, and the location is a person who never held the controls.

This is worth distinguishing from a different situation: the one where nobody has control, where the problem genuinely exceeds everyone's reach, and no amount of redistribution would help. That is tragic, and it calls for a different response. This piece is about the other case. The one where the control exists, is real, is exercised — and is not where the blame lands.

And now the guardrail, without which all of the above becomes an indulgence.

The responsibilization critique has a failure mode: it is so explanatory that it starts to explain everything, and then it stops distinguishing anything. Every appeal to responsibility begins to read as a neoliberal trick. Every call to resilience becomes an insult. At that point the analysis has become a permission slip — a sophisticated argument for not acting, which is just as immobilising as the guilt it replaced, and feels considerably more dignified.

Responsibility is not the property of neoliberalism. It has older and competing genealogies — reciprocity, kinship, care, civic obligation, solidarity — and the anthropologists Susanna Trnka and Catherine Trundle have made this argument at length in [*Competing Responsibilities*](https://www.dukeupress.edu/competing-responsibilities) (2017). In that volume, Nikolas Rose and Filippa Lentzos [argue specifically](https://doi.org/10.1515/9780822373056-002) that it is misleading to tie an ethopolitics of responsibility to neoliberalism at all — responsibility has been central to a long succession of ways of governing conduct, and treating it as neoliberalism's property concedes too much. Which suggests the more useful move: not to argue *against* responsibility and resilience but to fight for them on their own ground. To insist that resilience be built into systems rather than demanded of individuals, that responsibility travel with authority, that the words be reclaimed instead of abandoned.

Iris Marion Young's distinction is the practical one. What she calls the liability model looks backward and asks who caused this and who is at fault. Political responsibility — her social connection model — looks forward and asks who, with whom, can act on it now. Keeping the two apart lets you reject a false attribution of fault without surrendering your capacity to act, which is the whole trick.

## What a leader does instead

If you hand work to other people, the test runs in reverse. Three conditions for delegating responsibility rather than offloading it:

**Resource.** The means come with the task, or you specify what you will do about the gap. "Figure it out" is not a resource.

**Decision rights.** They can change the approach, not just execute yours. Accountability without latitude produces people who do the wrong thing carefully.

**The right to refuse.** There is a path to no that doesn't end a career. If there isn't, every yes you receive is uninformative.

Remove any one and the predictable thing happens. The team stops raising problems, because raising a problem now means owning it alone. Information stops flowing upward. You get a quiet organisation, which you will mistake for a healthy one until the quarter it isn't.

## Adulthood is knowing which is which

The common idea of maturity is taking on more. Carrying more weight, complaining less, absorbing what lands on you.

That definition is useful to almost everyone except the person holding it.

The better one: maturity is the capacity to tell your own from what was placed on you — and then to take the first, fully, and to put down the second. Both halves. Taking everything is not adulthood; it's absorption. Refusing everything is not adulthood either; it's a politics of being right while nothing moves.

The skill is the discrimination. Everything else follows from it.

## Why I keep at this anyway

A personal note, and the reason this piece exists at all.

Of everything I could spend my working life on, this is what I have chosen. [My practice](https://example.com/anne) is built on this one distinction, because it is the distinction that decides whether the people I work with are still in the field in five years.

They are the ones the mechanism reaches first. A planetary-scale problem, a team of six, no budget line for the part that actually matters, and an obligation taken on because nobody else was going to. They arrive saying *I'm not doing enough.* The first work is almost always establishing what *enough* could possibly mean, given what they actually hold.

So the claim is not that people doing good should recognise this mechanism and then step back. It is the reverse. Anyone whose work touches impact needs to be able to run those four questions, because the mechanism's reliable output is exhaustion, private shame, and a quiet exit from the field — and the people who leave almost always leave convinced the insufficiency was theirs. Seeing the structure is what makes it possible to stay.

And then to act regardless. Not because the structural critique is wrong — it isn't — but because the alternative is waiting for a counterparty who may never arrive. The gap between what the system promises and what it delivers is real, and someone will stand in it. Better that it be someone who knows what they are standing in, and chose it.

Who else, if not us.
