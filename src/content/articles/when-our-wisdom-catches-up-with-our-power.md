I have come to this conclusion slowly, and not by choice. For years I shared the hope that the fixes would arrive from outside — cleaner energy, smarter policy, more capital for impact, and now AI, which would help us govern the complexity we created. 2026 has made that hope hard to sustain. In January, the United States completed its second exit from the Paris Agreement.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> Development aid, already falling, was projected by the OECD to drop by another 9–17%.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> Sustainability, peacebuilding and development are losing money, legitimacy and attention at once — while capital floods into AI, even as a widely discussed MIT study found most corporate generative-AI pilots failing to deliver measurable returns.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> The crisis has reached the machinery of better-future-making itself.

It has reached us, too. Burnout, anxiety and loneliness are usually treated as private problems. I read them as "civilizational somatics" — nervous systems registering, quite accurately, conditions our social forms can no longer contain (WHO, 2022; Maté, 2022).

This is where I want to begin: not with the next technology, but with the people who will have to live through it.

## Exponential technologies, linear humans

My friend Boris Ryabov, a venture investor, insists that the falling cost of compute is the one fundamental driver of our future. The argument is seductive — legible, measurable, hard to attack. But technological exponentials behave like bacteria in a Petri dish: they grow until they hit the edge of the substrate. That substrate is a set of fundamentally linear human systems — institutions, culture, and human capabilities, the personal "hardware" and "software" of each of us. AI erodes the very cognitive and institutional foundations of coordination on which its own progress depends. What decides the future, then, is not the speed of technology but our "response-ability".

I describe civilization as four recursively coupled layers — consciousness, culture, governance and economy — with technology as a "crystallized culture"<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> that fixes them in material form (Luksha, 2023). This is why change in one layer rarely holds: new consciousness without corresponding institutions becomes private insight, while new institutions without corresponding human capacities are pulled back toward the logic they were meant to replace. Of all the boundaries the polycrisis trespasses, the one I keep returning to is the cognitive and spiritual "foundation": trust, the ability to comprehend complexity, collective sanity (Luksha, 2023). The ceiling is where the crisis is visible. The foundation is where it is decided.

The foundation runs through each of us. A person can undergo a genuine shift of worldview — recognize themselves as relational rather than separate, as participating in life rather than standing apart from it — and return on Monday morning to an incentive structure that rewards extraction. Over time, one of two things tends to happen: the new perception erodes back into compatibility with the conditions, or a widening dissonance sets in between what the person sees and what they are structurally able to do. Much of what we call burnout lives precisely in that gap.

## What does Planetary Adulthood ask of us?

In 1964 Margaret Mead wrote that we "stand at the centre of crisis with a new evolutionary device — our consciousness of the crisis — as our unique contribution" (Mead, 1964). Ervin Laszlo later framed the moment as a bifurcation leading either "up or out" (Laszlo, 1994). Six decades on, what matters is whether our recognition of the crisis becomes adequate to the responsibility it carries.

Humanity has acquired species-scale consequences without becoming a species-scale subject. I call the maturation of that subject — its capacities to perceive common conditions, deliberate across difference, remember, anticipate and learn — Planetary Adulthood. It is a functional question rather than a hierarchy of cultures: what must a technologically powerful civilization become capable of to remain viable within systems it can influence but never control?

We have rich languages for the development of the individual interior, and increasingly sophisticated languages for systemic complexity. What remains strangely underdeveloped is the space between them: the collective as a subject, with its own capacities for perception, memory and deliberation — and its own developmental failures. In work with Wendy Ellyatt, I explore this through the hypothesis of a "Symbiotic Transition": that climate disruption, technological acceleration, social fragmentation and psychological distress may be interacting expressions of a deeper reorganization of relationships among the Earth system, the biosphere, humanity and an increasingly consequential technosphere. It is an open hypothesis, not a prophecy. Tighter integration can generate new capacities — or dependency, domination and systemic fragility.

The answer starts with a change in agency, from control to participation, and runs through four shifts: from extraction to regeneration, from conflict to planetary solidarity, from atomization to relational care, from domination to symbiosis. Each requires something no policy, market or technology can supply alone — a different kind of human being inside the systems.

## The architecture we inherited

Every civilization gave its members a pre-configured inner architecture — a relationship to time, reward, meaning and belonging — through the rhythms of food production, initiations, crafts and rituals. Nobody chose it. The hunter's psyche was tuned for immediacy; the farmer's, and later the factory worker's, for patience and reliability. Each was coherent in its environment, and each breaks down outside it.

We are now in exactly that condition, with two disruptions at once. From below, AI and digital environments restore short-cycle dopamine loops to work, reversing millennia of suppression within a decade: we oscillate between acceleration and burnout inside the "AI tunnel", where attention narrows to the next quarter. From above, the polycrisis demands multigenerational thinking and coordination across timescales longer than any single life.

A second axis runs through the self. Modernity installed a competitive-separate self — bounded, defined by distinction rather than participation. It cannot carry a transition that asks for ecological embeddedness, encounter across difference and partnership with non-human intelligence; what is required is an ontological reorientation rather than a refinement of the modern self.

The risks run in both directions. A population that collapses into short-cycle dependency loses the capacity for long-duration coordination; one that overcorrects into suppressive discipline loses the creative velocity the transition demands. A population locked in competitive-separate selfhood cannot navigate its embeddedness in living systems; one that dissolves into pre-modern fusion loses the individual sovereignty and critical agency that a conscious transition requires.

There is no time for gradual adaptation. For the first time, the inner architecture that culture installs by default no longer fits the world people inhabit, and a new one has to be consciously built. I see this less as a burden than as an unprecedented form of agency — and far from a self-help problem: it is one of the foundational design challenges of the transition.

## The well-tempered clavier

In 1722 Bach completed the first book of *The Well-Tempered Clavier*. Before well-tempered tuning, a keyboard sounded beautiful in a few keys and out of tune in the rest. Bach showed that an instrument could be tuned so that every key was playable — none perfectly pure, all usable. The slight imperfection of each key was the structural cost of full-range availability.

The old inner architectures were pre-tempered instruments, magnificent in their native key. What the transition asks for is the capacity to move across registers without retuning — a core that stays stable not because it is rigid, but because it has been tempered. At its fullest this becomes polyphony: the sprint and the century-long arc, the sovereign self and the permeable self, played together on one instrument.

Think of a person living fully across very different roles — a mother, a priestess, a scholar, a dancer, a businesswoman — each role a different key, each relationship a different voice, all played by one tempered instrument. That is the specific demand of our moment, and it has no precedent.

## Six load-bearing functions

What would such an instrument consist of? My first structural hypothesis names six dimensions — not personality traits, but load-bearing functions without which human agency degrades under transition conditions, whatever one's intelligence or goodwill.

1. **A reward-independent anchor.** A stable observing self that does not depend on any reward cycle, near or far. Without it, every change of pace feels like loss: you are always accelerating or waiting, and waiting feels like deprivation.
2. **Somatic sovereignty.** The nervous system does not run on ideas. The body enforces reality through fatigue and biological cycles, holds the tension of many registers until it can resolve, and is our most direct form of ecological perception. Without it, we collapse not from wrong thinking but from depletion.
3. **Multi-scale meaning.** Most meaning systems are scale-locked: the specialist loses the why, the visionary the how, the activist the long arc. What is needed is the capacity to see one's contribution as fractal — a conversation serving a project, serving a civilizational arc, serving life on this planet — with the living world as the primary context of meaning rather than its backdrop.
4. **Timescale fluidity and epistemic patience.** Moving between sprint, endurance and stillness without losing identity — and holding an unresolved question over months or years. Modernity pathologized open loops; the transition turns well-formed uncertainty into infrastructure to be maintained.
5. **Relational coherence.** Remaining coherent while being genuinely changed by encounter — with other humans (the peace shift), living systems (the regenerative shift) and non-human intelligence (the post-anthropocentric shift). Contact that alters both sides and leaves both intact, rather than mere tolerance of difference. It begins at the most immediate scale: loving another person across real difference is already extraordinarily demanding.
6. **Sovereign orientation.** Action originating from one's own center rather than being reactive or captured — most needed in the gap between civilizational models, where many rush to fundamentalism, doom or nostalgia. It includes "grief and sunset literacy": the capacity to hospice exhausted identities and institutions without clinging or premature abandonment.

## A civilizational nervous system

A person with all six capacities, living inside a chronically frantic institution, is eventually crushed or forced out. If the individual dimensions tune the instruments, the collective ones design the concert hall. Universities, courts, religious communities and civic bodies were always also organs of memory, temporal buffering and collective affect regulation. I see five collective dimensions:

- **Civilizational memory** — carrying open questions across generations, and relay navigation: investing in work you will not see finished.
- **Institutionalized patience** — protected "slow rooms" where leaders think beyond the next quarter, and funding that rewards duration rather than speed.
- **Collective sprint and recovery** — mobilizing fast without letting emergency become the permanent operating mode.
- **Collective relational capacity** — holding genuine difference, including the standing of ecosystems and of generations not yet born.
- **Civilizational self-awareness** — universities, courts, foresight bodies and journalism able to see a society's state and notice when it has been captured by one timescale or one way of knowing.

Institutions also have a felt emotional tone, and it determines which decisions are possible. A school that is chronically anxious cannot transmit wisdom; a business that is permanently frantic cannot hold long cycles, whatever its stated strategy. Tending that tone — its pace, its quality of attention, its capacity for rest — is not soft culture work, but the substrate on which everything else depends.

## Beyond the Inner Development Goals

The closest existing framework is the Inner Development Goals — a serious, widely adopted map of inner capacities for global challenges (Inner Development Goals, 2021), on which this work builds. The difference is anthropological. IDG assumes a relatively stable self that acquires skills in service of sustainability goals. I assume the self itself is transformed — what kind of being a human becomes is the question. Hence five things outside IDG's brief: the temporal dimension, the somatic one, a relational ontology beyond interpersonal skill, sovereign orientation when the context itself has broken down, and a genuinely collective level.

The relationship is best read as nested layers of demand, each presupposing the one below:

| Layer | Framework | Question | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evolutionary transition | Inner architecture | How does the human instrument stay coherent and generative when the civilizational operating environment is breaking down? | Viability under phase transition |
| Global challenge response | Inner Development Goals | Which inner capacities enable effective contribution to sustainability goals? | Effectiveness and impact |
| Functional participation | Standard education | Which skills sustain participation in current systems? | Competence and productivity |

## Where the architecture takes hold

An open transition cannot be engineered from a blueprint. I borrow Kevin Kelly's word "protopia" (Kelly, 2011) for another mode of agency: creating conditions in which better configurations can emerge, be tested against reality and grow. New forms appear first as "islands of coherence", where several layers reinforce one another — a different economy supported by different governance, culture and ways of seeing. Their importance lies not in being perfect models, but in making an abstract possibility operational: they let a civilization learn from reality rather than from ideology.

Islands become consequential when they connect into "archipelagos" — through finance, law, knowledge commons and, above all, learning ecosystems: webs of relationships built on trust and psychological safety (Luksha et al., 2018; Spencer-Keyse, Luksha & Cubista, 2020). There, inner architecture and civilizational infrastructure can evolve together — and they must. A regenerative economy cannot endure if the people inside it still operate through short-term extraction; peaceful governance cannot be sustained by people unable to remain in relationship across deep difference.

Protopian transition is neither central planning nor spontaneous emergence. It asks leaders to act as custodians of coordination capacity rather than its controllers: sensing what is becoming possible, protecting promising forms, connecting experiments across scales. The pathways into this architecture — its initiations — remain an open design challenge.

## The instrument of evolution

The most comforting hope of the past decades was the promise of a rescuer. There is no "savior from above" — no deus ex machina, technological, political or metaphysical. The absence of a savior is not a tragedy — it is the condition under which collective responsibility finally becomes unavoidable.

The tension we feel is not a malfunction; it is the string under the right pressure, which makes sound possible. The dissonance of our moment is the price of becoming an instrument that can play registers never played before. Planetary Adulthood is not a destination, but the ongoing practice of becoming the instrument evolution needs. The music has already begun. The question is whether we will be tuned to play it.
